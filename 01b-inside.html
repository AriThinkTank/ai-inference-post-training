Inside the Machine

Chapter 01 said the model scores every possible next word. This chapter opens the box and shows how, and what happens to those scores afterwards.

Skippable if you only care about policy. Worth twenty minutes if you want to know why the words temperature, attention and context window mean what they mean, because they turn up constantly and are almost never explained.

I.01

Attention is a very good filing system

01
IraWhen you get to the end of a long sentence, how do you remember what it was about?
02
EchoI don't remember. I look. Every word can reach back and read every earlier word directly, in one hop. Distance costs me nothing.
03 Three questions

Each word writes a query — what am I looking for. Each word also puts out a key — here's what I am. And a value — here's what I've got. Match the queries against the keys and you know who should listen to whom.

that's it. that's attention.
Query, key, value: one word deciding what to listen to Simplified. Real attention scales the match scores and passes them through a softmax before they become weights. Mechanism: Vaswani et al., arXiv:1706.03762.
Why this was such a big deal in 2017The models before it read left to right, one word at a time, and the beginning of a sentence faded as you went. Attention throws that out. Every position sees every earlier position at full strength, and the whole thing computes in parallel, which is what made training on the entire internet possible at all.

And why it costs what it does

Every word looking at every other word means the work grows with the square of the length. Double the context, quadruple the attention. Nearly every efficiency trick in the last five years — flash attention, grouped-query attention, sliding windows — is an attempt to get out from under that square.

Why depth helps

Early layers do grammar and word identity. Middle layers do syntax and reference. Late layers do task and intent. Nobody assigned that division of labour. It shows up on its own.

The residual stream

Each layer adds to a running total rather than overwriting it. Without that, stacks this deep simply fail to train. It also gives interpretability researchers a place to read from.

I.03 · The bit everyone gets wrong

What temperature, k and p actually do

These three settings sit in every API and are described in documentation as "controls creativity", which explains nothing. Here is the arithmetic, one stage at a time. Move a dial and the affected stage lights up.

The pipeline, before you play with it Order matters: truncation happens after the probabilities exist, not before. Sources: Vaswani et al., arXiv:1706.03762; Holtzman et al., arXiv:1904.09751.
Top-k

k is a headcount

"Keep the best 40." Always 40, whether the model is certain or hasn't a clue. When it's certain, you've let 39 bad options stay in the running. When it's lost, you've thrown away good ones. Blunt, but easy to reason about.

Top-p

p is a share of the probability

"Keep whatever it takes to cover 90% of the belief." Sometimes that's two tokens, sometimes two hundred. It adapts to how sure the model is, which is why it's the default nearly everywhere and why it's also called nucleus sampling.

Temperature 0 is a lie people tellDividing by zero isn't a thing. What providers actually do at "temperature 0" is skip the dice entirely and take the highest-scoring token every time. That's greedy decoding, and it isn't quite deterministic either: batching and floating-point ordering on a GPU can still shuffle the result between runs. If you've ever seen "temperature 0" produce two different answers, that's why.
I.04

Six more settings you'll meet in an API

None of these touch the weights either. All of them change what you get.

01

Repetition penalty

Docks points from tokens that have already appeared. Fixes the model getting stuck in a loop. Push it too far and it starts avoiding words it genuinely needs, like the subject of the sentence.

02

Max tokens

A hard stop. The model doesn't wrap up gracefully when it hits this, it just stops mid-word. If replies keep ending abruptly, this is usually why.

03

Stop sequences

Text that ends generation on sight. Useful when you're using the model inside a larger format and need it to stay in its lane.

04

Seed

Fixes the dice so the same request gives the same reply. Providers describe this as best-effort, for the floating-point reasons above.

05

Constrained decoding

Blank out every token that would break a grammar, so the output is valid JSON by construction rather than by hope. This is how "guaranteed structured output" works, and it's the sampler doing it, not the model.

06

Logit bias

Add or subtract a fixed amount from named tokens before anything else. Crude, powerful, and the bluntest content control there is.

The point of this section

A great deal of what looks like model behaviour is sampler behaviour. Before concluding that a model is creative, repetitive, terse or unreliable, find out what these six were set to. Usually nobody wrote it down.

I.05

Why models are the size they are

01
2020: bigger is better

Performance improved smoothly and predictably with size, data and compute. The industry read this as: build the largest model you can afford.

02
2022: Chinchilla

DeepMind showed that for a fixed compute budget, most models of the era were far too large for the amount of data they'd been fed. A smaller model trained on more text beat a bigger one.

everyone's model was the wrong shape
03
2024: serving wins

You train a model once and serve it billions of times. So it became worth over-training a small model well past the compute-optimal point, purely to make it cheap to run.

04 2026: a third axis

Now there's compute at training time and compute at answer time, and they trade against each other. How they trade is one of the four things nobody has pinned down.

Why any of this matters to a policy reader

Compute thresholds assume a stable relationship between compute and capability. Scaling laws are what makes that assumption look reasonable. But the shape of the curve has been revised twice in five years, and a third variable arrived in 2025.

A rule calibrated to the 2022 understanding of scaling is not calibrated to the 2026 one.
