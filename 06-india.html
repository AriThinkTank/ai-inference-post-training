Where India Actually Stands

Three findings, and each one cuts against something the Indian AI conversation currently assumes.

First: India's compute problem is not a shortage. Second: the data centre constraint is not megawatts. Third: the thing India is missing most has no export control on it, because nobody sells it. Everything below is worked out from named sources with the arithmetic printed, and every dataset is downloadable.

N.01

What each stage actually needs, in hardware and software

The three stages look similar from outside and have almost nothing in common as procurement problems. Pretraining needs a single tightly coupled machine. Inference needs many loosely coupled ones. Post-training sits in between and needs something neither of them does: data and environments.

The row that decides everythingLook at "tolerates failure". Pretraining needs one enormous reliable machine, which is a hard engineering and procurement problem. Inference needs lots of small unreliable ones, which is a problem India already solves at scale every day. That single difference is why the strategy in this chapter points where it does.
N.02 · The original analysis

Concentration and control are two different things

Almost every supply chain analysis stops at "this market has few suppliers". That is only half the question. A market can be extremely concentrated and pose no strategic risk at all, and a market can be only moderately concentrated and be shut off in a fortnight. So we scored every input twice.

Axis one

How few suppliers

Measured with the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, the same tool competition regulators use for merger review. Sum the squared market shares. A monopoly scores 10,000.

Axis two

How switchable-off

Our own 1 to 5 scale. Does a control instrument already exist? Is the product licensed rather than sold? Can it be forked? Is delivery metered, so revocation is instant?

Two results worth the whole exercise

PyTorch and CUDA are equally concentrated and not remotely equally dangerous. PyTorch scores 4,113 on HHI, CUDA 8,136. Both are dominant. But PyTorch is permissively licensed and forkable, so its control leverage is 1. CUDA is proprietary and revocable, so its leverage is 4. Any analysis that ranks risk by market share alone gets this backwards.

EDA is the least concentrated of the serious chokepoints and the most controllable. HHI of 2,093, which is only moderate. But the tools are cloud-delivered and licence-metered, so an entity listing takes effect within one billing cycle. Low concentration, maximum leverage.

The quiet one

Verifiable-reward environments score 6,400 and sit at leverage 3. There is no export control on them and there never will be, because they are not sold. They are built in house and kept there. You cannot be denied something nobody offers, which sounds like good news and is the opposite.

N.03 · The finding

What 38,000 GPUs can actually do

India has 38,000 accelerators empanelled under the IndiaAI Mission as of December 2025, with a stated target of 100,000 by the end of 2026. Nobody seems to have converted that into work. So we did, using the disclosed GPU-hours from DeepSeek's own technical reports as the yardstick.

N.04 · The second finding

India is not short of megawatts. It is short of kilowatts per rack.

Every report on Indian AI infrastructure quotes total data centre capacity in gigawatts. It is the wrong unit. An accelerator rack draws between 40 and 130 kilowatts. A typical Indian colocation hall was designed for 5 to 10. The megawatts are there. The halls that can absorb them in one rack are not.

How to read that gap

It does not mean the projection is wrong. Capital commitments have genuinely stepped up, and 2025 added a record 440 MW. It does mean the projection assumes a discontinuity, not a trend, and anyone planning against it should say which discontinuity they are relying on. With four observations we can fit a growth rate; we cannot do inference, and we have not pretended to.

A number worth being sceptical about

"India has 20% of the world's chip design workforce"

This appears in ministerial statements, industry reports and roughly every article on Indian semiconductors, including several published this month. We tried to trace it to a primary source and could not. There is no published methodology, no denominator, and no definition of who counts as a design engineer.

It may well be roughly right. India plainly has deep design capability, and the global captive centres are real. But a figure this load-bearing should have a source, and repeating it without one is how policy gets built on sand. We have graded it D and used it nowhere in the calculations on this page.

N.06

Which export control bites at which stage

India is not an embargoed destination for any of these. That is the important starting point, and it is often lost. The exposure is second-order: queue position, pricing power, compliance burden, and the standing possibility that the rules change. Two of the instruments below have already been imposed and withdrawn once.

The thing to plan against

Not a ban. Volatility. The rules moved four times in eighteen months, in both directions. Predictability is the commodity India is actually short of.

04 Which changes the ask

If the problem were denial, the answer would be indigenisation. Because the problem is volatility, the answer is duration: multi-year commitments, second sources, and stages that need less of the volatile input.

N.07

Four dials, and what they do

Not a forecast, and it has no probabilities in it. It is a way of finding out which variable your conclusion is actually resting on. The arithmetic prints under the result so you can disagree with the specific number rather than the general vibe.

Scenario we ran: the expensive mistake

Build dial to maximum, environments dial to zero. You end up with 100,000 accelerators, high-density halls, and nothing distinctive to run on them. A very costly way to serve other people's models.

Scenario we ran: the exposed case

Licensing tightens while the domestic build is still on trend. Training capacity collapses. Serving capacity barely moves. That asymmetry is the single strongest argument for weighting inference now.

Scenario we ran: the interesting case

Modest hardware, serious environments. India ends up exporting maintained evaluation and reward environments for languages and domains nobody else covers. Small budget, genuine leverage, no import licence required.

N.08 · Options, part one

Geopolitical and strategic options

These are things India would do with other countries and firms. They are options, not recommendations, and each carries a cost we have tried to state honestly.

G.01 Buy duration rather than volume The scarce commodity is predictability, not chips. A multi-year supply undertaking with defined volumes, negotiated government to government, is worth more than a larger one-off allocation. Cost: locks India into a vendor generation and reduces bargaining room later.
G.02 Go around the sharpest chokepoint, not through it Accelerators score 7,793 on HHI at leverage 5. Assembly and packaging score 1,444 at leverage 1. India already has three operating ATMP plants. Advanced packaging capacity is queue-constrained rather than licence-constrained, which makes it the one hardware layer where money alone buys entry. Cost: packaging is capital-hungry and India would be a late entrant against incumbents with a decade of yield learning.
G.03 Treat HBM as the thing to secure, not logic Three suppliers, sold out through 2026, on the control list since December 2024. Micron already packages memory at Sanand. Deepening that relationship into HBM assembly is a narrower and more achievable ask than a leading-edge fab. Cost: back-end memory packaging is not front-end memory fabrication, and the gap between them is larger than it looks.
G.04 Build the coalition nobody has built: the middle tier The diffusion rule created a category of countries with capital, engineers and no leading-edge fab. India, the Gulf states, Japan on some inputs, parts of Europe. That group has never negotiated together on compute access and has more combined purchasing power than any of them alone. Cost: members have incompatible relationships with Washington, and the coalition would be read as a bloc.
G.05 Export environments, not engineers The one input with no supplier and no control instrument. Whoever builds maintained verifiable-reward environments for law, medicine, agriculture and Indian-language reasoning owns something the frontier labs need and cannot buy. India has the domain institutions and the annotation base. This is the only item on the list that creates leverage rather than reducing exposure. Cost: requires sustained institutional funding of unglamorous work with no announceable groundbreaking.
G.06 Hedge on open weights deliberately, and say so Licences bind future releases; weights already downloaded cannot be recalled. A stated national policy of maintaining local mirrors of significant open releases costs almost nothing and removes an entire class of disruption. Cost: open weights trail the frontier, and depending on them means accepting a capability lag.
N.09 · Options, part two

Domestic policy options

These India can do alone, and most of them are cheap. Several are administrative rather than budgetary, which is unusual in this area.

P.01 Publish cluster topology, not just GPU counts "38,000 GPUs" is not a capability statement. The largest contiguous, high-bandwidth-connected block is. Requiring empanelled providers to report it would cost nothing and would immediately reveal whether India has a training machine or a serving estate. On current evidence it is the second. Effort: a reporting line in the existing empanelment contract.
P.02 One anchor cluster instead of more distributed capacity Fragmentation, not scarcity, is what stops India pretraining. A single reserved block of a few thousand tightly coupled accelerators, bookable in month-long slots, would change what is possible more than another 20,000 cards spread across fourteen providers. Effort: a scheduling and procurement decision, not new money.
P.03 Fund environments the way ISRO funds test facilities Verifiable-reward environments are infrastructure, not research output. They need decade-long maintenance funding, staff who are not judged on papers, and domain institutions in the loop. India has the model for this in its space and standards bodies and has never applied it to software. Effort: small budget, hard institutional design.
P.04 Write high-density into data centre policy now Infrastructure status and state incentives are written around megawatts and floor area. Neither predicts whether a hall can take a 120 kW rack. Tying incentives to rack density and liquid-cooling readiness would redirect private capital at zero fiscal cost. Effort: amend existing incentive criteria.
P.05 Disclose the answer-time budget in any public evaluation Chapter 04 argued that a benchmark score without a compute budget is not comparable to anything. India funds model development and will increasingly evaluate models. Requiring the budget alongside the score, on anything publicly funded, is free and would put India ahead of most regulators on a point they have all missed. Effort: one clause in grant conditions.
P.06 Stop citing the 20% design workforce figure until someone sources it It may be right. Nobody has shown the working, and it is now load-bearing in strategy documents. Commissioning a proper labour-market census of the Indian semiconductor and AI workforce would cost a rounding error against the ISM outlay and would replace a slogan with a baseline. Effort: one survey.
Notice what is not on this listA leading-edge fab. Not because it would not be useful, but because nothing in the analysis above suggests it is the binding constraint on anything India is trying to do in the next five years. It is the most expensive answer to a question the data does not ask.
N.10

How every number here was worked out

If you disagree with a conclusion, the arithmetic below is where to attack it. All four datasets are plain CSV and can be edited and reloaded.

Datasets

Download and check

  • india-compute.csv — fleet, capacity, trade
  • india-position.csv — capability scores with evidence
  • export-controls.csv — instruments and effects
Source grades

What the letters mean

A — primary: government release, company filing, technical report with method.
B — reputable secondary with stated method.
C — industry estimate, method not published.
D — widely repeated, primary source not traceable. Used for context only, never in a calculation.

HHI
Sum of squared percentage market shares, 0 to 10,000, as used in competition review. Only genuinely single-firm shares are squared. Unnamed residual share is modelled as maximally fragmented and contributes nothing, so every HHI on this page is a lower bound on true concentration, not a point estimate. Shares are from Counterpoint Research (HBM, DRAM), company filings and industry revenue estimates as listed per row.
Control leverage
Our own 1 to 5 scale, and the most contestable thing here. 1: no control instrument, product is open or forkable. 2: no instrument today but plausible. 3: no instrument, but supply is allocated by contract and queueing is severe. 4: an instrument exists and has been used. 5: an instrument exists, has been used, and takes effect without any shipment being stopped. The scores are judgement calls; the basis for each is in the CSV so you can rescore them.
Stage exposure index
For each stage, take the inputs that stage uses, normalise HHI to 0-1 and leverage to 0-1, combine with the weights the reader sets, discount inputs scoring leverage 1 by up to 60% for openness, then take the unweighted mean across inputs. An ordinal comparison between stages, nothing more. It is not a probability and should never be quoted as one.
Fleet throughput
38,000 accelerators (MeitY/IndiaAI, Dec 2025) × 989.5 TFLOP/s dense BF16 (NVIDIA H100 SXM datasheet) × 40% model FLOP utilisation = 1.50 × 1019 FLOP/s, or 1.30 × 1024 FLOP per day. GPU-hours per day = 38,000 × 24 = 912,000. The fleet is mixed in reality; treating it as H100-equivalent is a simplification stated here and adjustable in the calculator.
The DeepSeek comparisons
2,788,000 GPU-hours for V3 pretraining and 147,000 for R1 reinforcement learning, both as disclosed in the respective technical reports. Divided by 912,000 GPU-hours per day gives 3.06 days and 3.9 hours. These are the cleanest published frontier figures available; almost no other lab discloses them, and one lab's numbers should not be generalised.
Serving capacity
312 aggregate output tokens per second per accelerator for a 70-billion-parameter model under continuous batching, derived from a commonly reported 2,500 tokens/s across an 8-way tensor-parallel group. 38,000 × 312 = 11.9 million tokens/s, or 1.02 × 1012 per day. At 5,000 output tokens per user per day that is 205 million daily users. Order of magnitude, not a service-level guarantee: real throughput varies several-fold with model size, quantisation, context length and latency target.
Power and rack density
700 W per accelerator (H100 SXM datasheet), PUE 1.5. 38,000 GPUs = 27 MW IT load, 40 MW total; 100,000 = 70 MW IT, 105 MW total. National colocation IT load 1,600 MW (JLL, June 2026), giving 1.7% and 4.4%. Racks assume the hall density divided by 0.7 kW per accelerator, and 200 racks per hall. Modern reference point: NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 at 72 accelerators and roughly 120 kW per rack.
Growth rates
Compound annual growth computed directly from endpoints: (1,600/520)1/6 − 1 = 20.6% for 2020-2026, and (1,500/520)1/5 − 1 = 23.6% for 2020-2025. The 2029 requirement is (6,000/1,600)1/3 − 1 = 55.4%. These are fitted growth rates, not forecasts. With four observations a regression would produce confidence intervals with no meaning, so we have not run one. Anyone who quotes a t-statistic on this series is selling something.
Scenario engine
Multiplicative, with every multiplier printed under the output. Domestic build 0.9-2.63× (2.63 is the stated 100,000 target over 38,000), licensing posture 1.15-0.35×, supply 1.1-0.55×. The housing discount is 0.35 unless high-density halls are selected. Environment readiness is scored but deliberately does not enter the hardware arithmetic, because it cannot be bought with accelerators. The multipliers are illustrative and chosen to span a plausible range; they are not estimated from anything.
What we could not do

We could not get firm-level Indian GPU deployment by provider, because it is not published. We could not verify installed-versus-empanelled counts beyond mid-2025. We could not source the 20% design workforce claim. And the control leverage scale is ours, which means the exposure index inherits our judgement about what counts as controllable. Those four gaps are where this analysis would change most if better data appeared.

Chapter 06, in one line

India has been counting the input it has most of, and not counting the one it has none of.

Accelerators are announceable. Environments are not. The arithmetic says the second matters more.
