Three More Ways to Look at It

Cost tells you what a stage buys. It does not tell you who keeps the money, whether the thing you care about is genuinely irreplaceable, or what a country could build if it decided to.

These three views answer those questions. The first uses company filings rather than our own estimates, so it is the best-sourced analysis on the whole dashboard. The second puts three dimensions on screen at once, because every earlier chart had to drop one. The third is the softest and the most practical: how long, and how much.

A.01 · From the filings

The layers that pay best are the ones you cannot enter

Everything else on this dashboard measures cost: what gets spent, and on what. This measures the other side of the same transaction. When a rupee moves through the AI supply chain, who keeps it?

The answer comes from audited accounts rather than from our estimates, which makes it the firmest thing here. NVIDIA, TSMC and SK hynix all publish. What they publish is startling, and it lines up against India’s options in a way that is worth sitting with for a moment.

The number that should have been impossible

SK hynix reported a 76 per cent operating margin in the second quarter of 2026. Memory was supposed to be the commodity corner of semiconductors: brutal cycles, thin margins, everyone selling the same thing. For one quarter at least, memory out-earned both the most advanced logic foundry on earth and the company that designs the chips.

What changed is that high-bandwidth memory is not a commodity. There are three suppliers, demand exceeds supply, and the business has moved onto long-term agreements with about ten customers. That is not a spot market. It is closer to a utility contract, and it prices like one.

Export controls cost the seller too

NVIDIA’s gross margin fell from 75.0 to 71.1 per cent between fiscal 2025 and 2026. A large part of that was a $4.5bn charge on H20 inventory stranded when US licensing requirements landed in April 2025, plus a further $8bn of expected revenue the company told investors it would not book.

Worth holding on to when reading about controls as though they are costless to the country imposing them. They are not. That is also why the rules keep moving.

The pattern, stated plainlySort the table by margin and the top three layers are closed to India. Sort it by how open each layer is and the top three all pay 40 per cent or less. That inverse relationship is not a coincidence: the layers that pay best are protected by monopoly or duopoly, and a monopoly is by definition not accepting applications. The one place the pattern breaks is human data, which pays decently and is wide open, because its barrier to entry is a supply of credentialed people rather than a fabrication plant.
A.02 · Three dimensions

Every earlier chart had to drop something

A cost line only matters strategically if three things are true at once. It has to be expensive, or losing it does not hurt. It has to be concentrated, or somebody else will sell it to you. And it has to be hard to substitute, or you simply use the next best thing.

That is a three-dimensional question, and a flat chart can only show two of them. So here is the third. Drag the box to turn it. Rotate until the far corner faces you, and count how many points are actually in it.

What to look for, first

The corner with high cost, high concentration and low substitutability. Two points sit there. One is the pretraining accelerator line, which is exactly what export controls were designed around. The other is reinforcement learning environments, which has no control instrument at all.

What to look for, second

How far the inference accelerator point sits from the pretraining one, despite being the same physical product. The only thing separating them is substitutability, and that single judgement is what the sensitivity test on the workings page identified as carrying the whole conclusion.

What to look for, third

The cluster near the origin on the concentration axis: staff, energy, buildings. Large costs, no concentration, nothing to restrict. Roughly a third to four-fifths of every stage’s bill lives in that harmless region, and it almost never gets discussed.

A.03 · Time and money

What could India actually build, and by when

Knowing where the chokepoints are is only useful if you also know which of them a country could do something about. So the last view puts every alternative on two axes that policymakers actually work in: how many years, and how much capital.

Set a budget and a deadline. Green points are reachable, red ones are not. These are our estimates rather than costings, and they are the softest numbers on this dashboard, but the ordering is more robust than the absolute values and the ordering is the point.

Notice what is cheap

Mirroring open weights costs about a million dollars and takes a month, and it removes an entire category of disruption risk. Forking a serving engine is a weekend. Building a national annotation capability across 22 languages is a year and forty million dollars, which is less than a single mid-sized government IT programme.

Reinforcement learning environments are the largest item on the reachable list: two years and sixty million dollars for something that carries a quarter of the post-training bill and has no supplier anywhere in the world. On any reasonable reading of this dashboard that is the best-value item on the board.

And what stays out of reach at any budget

Leading-edge logic fabrication sits at twelve years and thirty billion dollars, and even that assumes equipment India cannot currently import at the nodes required. The binding constraint there is not capital. It is somebody else’s export policy, which means no Indian budget decision changes the timeline.

High-bandwidth memory fabrication is nearly as bad, and it is worth separating from the back-end packaging India already does at Sanand. Those are different industries that share a word.

A.04 · The whole argument in one chart

Watch the two curves come apart

If you take one image away from this dashboard, make it this one. Press play and watch six years pass. The wine line is what it costs to build the frontier. The marigold line is what it costs to buy capability that was frontier-grade in 2020. They start in the same industry and end in different ones.

Why this settles the strategic questionA country that cannot afford the wine line is not losing. It is being handed the marigold line, for free, every year, by the same laboratories it cannot compete with. The only way to lose that bargain is to spend the budget chasing the wine line anyway.

Pretraining buys hardware.
Post-training buys people.
Inference buys buildings and power.
