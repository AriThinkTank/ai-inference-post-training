Four Teachers

Four ways to teach a model, applied in order. Each one is cheaper than the last, and each one is worse at its job than the last.

That trade is the whole story. You start with a human writing the perfect answer, which is slow and expensive. You end with a program that runs the code and says pass or fail, which is instant and free, and can only mark the small fraction of tasks a program can check.

B.00 · The shape of the chapter

The marker gets weaker at every step

Climb the staircase and watch who is doing the marking. A human writing the answer. A human ranking answers. A model imitating that human. Then no human at all.

B.01 · Teacher one

Copying: show it what good looks like

01
Supervised fine-tuning. About ten thousand examples, each one written by a person.

A prompt, and the answer somebody wanted. That's the whole dataset.

02
IraJust copy these? That's it?
EchoCopy the answers. Not the questions. Very much not the questions.
03 Ten thousand, not ten million

This stage is startlingly cheap next to pretraining. It's also where quality beats quantity by a mile: a small set of genuinely good answers outperforms a huge set of mediocre ones, and every lab now guards its SFT data more carefully than its architecture.

What it teaches

Format. When to stop. What register to use. How to call a tool. What a refusal sounds like. All the manners chapter 01 said were missing.

What it can't teach

Judgement. It can only copy the examples it was given, so it inherits every blind spot in the dataset without knowing it has them.

Two cheats worth knowing

LoRA trains a small patch instead of the whole model, cutting the bill enormously. Rejection sampling has the model generate its own answers, keeps the good ones, and trains on those.

Mechanism: Ouyang et al., arXiv:2203.02155. LoRA: arXiv:2106.09685.
B.02 · Teacher two

Ranking: two answers, pick the better one

Writing a perfect answer is hard. Saying which of two answers is better is easy. That asymmetry is the entire idea behind preference training, and it's a good one.

The problem it runs into immediately

Humans are slow. Training needs millions of comparisons and you cannot buy millions of careful human judgements. So you train a second model to imitate the humans, and use that instead.

That second model is the reward model. Meet Mark.

Mark, the stand-inTrained on a few hundred thousand human comparisons. Reproduces roughly seven in ten of them. The other three are where things go wrong.
ApproachHow it worksWhere it's used
RLHFHumans rank, a reward model imitates them, the model is trained to score well against itThe original. Still used where quality matters most
DPOSkips the reward model entirely and folds the preference straight into the lossNow the default for most open releases. Simpler and much cheaper
RLAIFA model does the ranking instead of a human, often against written principlesEverywhere, because human comparisons don't scale
Ouyang et al., arXiv:2203.02155; Rafailov et al., arXiv:2305.18290; Bai et al., arXiv:2212.08073.

04 Goodhart's law

Once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Charles Goodhart said it about monetary policy in 1975. It has never been more literally true than here.

Caught in the wild

Padding for length. Agreeing with whatever the user said. Headings that perform rigour. Confident tone where the honest answer is "I don't know".

What's done about it

A drift penalty keeps the model near where it started. Reward models get retrained mid-run. Multiple judges get averaged. None of it solves the problem.

The bit for policy readers

Model behaviour that looks like a personality choice is often a scoring artefact. Sycophancy isn't a value someone picked. It's what happens when a stand-in rewards agreement.

Documented cases and mitigations: Lambert, rlhfbook.com, chs. 14 and 15.
B.04 · Teacher three

Checking: sack the judge, run the code

01
IraWhy do we need Mark at all? Just run the program and see if it works.
02
VeraTest suite: 47 of 47 passed.

No opinion. No taste. No way to flatter her.

03 This changed everything

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, or RLVR. Nothing can game it, so you can crank the optimisation as hard as you like. It's the single biggest reason models got dramatically better at maths and code between 2024 and 2026, and barely moved on essay writing.

the catch is on the next line
04
The catch

Vera can only mark what a program can check. That turns out to be a narrow slice of the things people actually want done.

The uncomfortable implication

Capability now grows fastest where checking is easy. Maths, competitive programming, formal proofs. It grows slowly where checking is hard, which includes nearly everything a policy analyst, a doctor or a teacher does all day.

This isn't a claim about what's important. It's a claim about where the training signal exists, and the two have come apart.

Try asking Vera to mark an essayShe can check the word count and whether it cites a real source. She cannot tell you whether the argument holds. Nobody has built a checker that can.
DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1, 2025; Lambert, rlhfbook.com, ch. 7.

EchoNobody. Longer working passed the checker more often, so longer working is what survived.
04 Emergent, genuinely

Self-correction wasn't designed, demonstrated or rewarded directly. It fell out of rewarding correct answers. This is one of the few places where "emergent" is used honestly.

And it costs you money

Those chains of working are tokens. You pay for them. Some providers hide the reasoning from you and bill you for it anyway, which is worth knowing when you read a price per million tokens.

It also means the same model can be run cheaply and stupidly or expensively and well. That dial lives in chapter 03, and it is the awkward fact underneath every compute threshold.

The training algorithm

GRPO

Generate several answers, use the group's own average as the bar. Removes the extra value network that PPO needs, which is why it spread so fast.

DeepSeekMath, arXiv:2402.03300; DeepSeek-AI, DeepSeek-R1, 2025.
B.06 · Teacher four

Doing: forty steps, one mark at the end

Agentic training is the current frontier and the hardest of the four by some distance. The model uses tools, reads files, runs code and works towards a goal over many steps. At the end, it either got there or it didn't.

Two things make this hard

Credit assignment. The task failed. Which of the forty steps was to blame? Step four looked fine at the time and doomed everything after it. There is no clean answer to this, and it is the bottleneck.

Environments. You need a real sandbox with real tools that behaves consistently across millions of runs. Building and maintaining those is engineering work, not research, and it doesn't get published.

Why this matters for diffusionWeights leak. Papers get published. Environments don't travel. That's why agentic ability has spread far more slowly than reasoning ability, and it's the most under-discussed asymmetry in the field.
A number worth carrying

~5%

Post-training as a share of pretraining, for this one model

Do not generalise from this. It's one lab, one model, one year, and it's the only frontier-scale figure anyone has published.

The replication problem

A single ablation at this scale costs tens of thousands of GPU hours. Almost no university can afford to check anyone's claims, so most of what we know about post-training comes from the people selling it.

B.07 · After the teaching

Shrinking it: the part that makes capability travel

01
Distillation

Ask the big model a few hundred thousand questions. Train a small model on its answers, reasoning and all. The small one picks up much of the behaviour.

02
IraSo you can copy a model without ever seeing inside it?
EchoOn the tasks you copied, mostly yes. Wander off those and the copy falls apart quickly.
03 This is why capability spreads

Every provider's terms forbid it. It happens anyway, and it is close to unprovable from the outside. Between distillation and quantisation, the gap between a frontier release and something similar running on ordinary hardware is now measured in months.

The bit that should interest anyone writing export policy

Four-bit weights put a 70-billion-parameter model on hardware nobody is tracking. The controls were designed around the machines that build models, not the machines that run them, and compression keeps moving the second category downmarket.

GPTQ, arXiv:2210.17323; distillation results in DeepSeek-R1, 2025.
B.08

All four, on one page

TeacherWho marksWhat it's good atHow it fails
CopyingA person, writing the answerFormat, tone, stopping, tool syntaxInherits every blind spot in the dataset
RankingA person choosing, then a model imitating themTaste, helpfulness, things nobody can specifyThe stand-in gets gamed. Padding and flattery
CheckingA programMaths, code, proofs, anything with an answer keyOnly works where a checker exists, which is a narrow slice
DoingA program, once, at the very endLong tasks with tools, real workNobody knows which of the forty steps was wrong
The thread running through all four

Every stage replaces an expensive marker with a cheaper one. Each substitution buys scale and loses fidelity. Most of the odd behaviour people notice in deployed models is a fingerprint of one of those substitutions.
