Chapter 02 · Post-training

Four Teachers

Four stages, each learning from a different kind of signal. The signal is the thing to watch: it decides what the stage can teach, what it costs, and how it fails. Moving up the stack, the teacher gets cheaper to consult and harder to trust.

B.01 · The shape of the chapter

Each stage sits on top of the one below it

None of them replaces its predecessor. Each narrows the behaviour of the one beneath.

The one sentence to remember

A human demonstrator is expensive and reliable. A learned reward model is cheap and gameable. An automatic checker is cheap and reliable but only exists for a narrow set of tasks. An environment is reliable, expensive, and closer to infrastructure than to a dataset.

The four supervision signals, compared

This table is the analytical core of the chapter. When a new method is announced, the first question worth asking is which column it belongs in, because that determines what it can and cannot fix.

B.02 · Stage 1 · Demonstrations

Show the answer, copy the answer

01
Somebody writes out thousands of good answers by hand
IraHere. This is what a good answer looks like. Copy it.
02
EchoCopied. Also I have stopped at the end, which is new for me.
03
IraCan you get better than me at this?
EchoNo. I am copying you. If you are wrong, I learn the mistake with the same enthusiasm.
04
The ceiling

A model trained only to copy demonstrations cannot reliably become better than the people who wrote them. Every stage after this one exists to break that ceiling. It is worth holding on to, because it explains why the field did not simply write more examples and stop.

The ceiling
05
Still the most visible stage

The most ordinary step in the pipeline, and still the one that does the most work a user can see. Almost everything you perceive as the model’s personality begins here.

Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 4 (instruction fine-tuning) and ch. 9 (rejection sampling). Dialogue is invented; the ceiling it describes is the standard result for imitation learning.
V.01

Loss masking

Only response tokens are scored. Without it, the model learns to generate prompts as readily as answers.

V.02

Parameter-efficient variants

LoRA and similar methods train a small set of added weights instead of all of them, which is what makes downstream fine-tuning affordable for an organisation that did not build the model.

V.03

Rejection sampling

Generate many candidates, keep those a filter approves, and fine-tune on them. A cheap halfway house to reinforcement learning.

Six behaviours that appear during this stage

01

Answering the question asked

The single largest visible change. A base model continues the prompt; a fine-tuned model responds to it.

02

Ending the reply

The model learns to emit a stop token rather than continuing until it runs out of budget.

03

A consistent register

Length, structure, hedging, use of headings. Almost all of what a user perceives as personality begins here.

04

Refusal patterns

How to decline, and in what tone. Whether the refusal is well judged is decided later, in preference training.

05

Tool and function-call syntax

Emitting a structured call in the exact format an external system will parse, without being shown the schema each time.

06

Reasoning format

Where a reasoning model is being built, this stage teaches the shape of a long working-out. Stage 3 teaches it to be correct.

A useful diagnostic

If a complaint about a model is about how it says something, the answer usually lies in stages 1 and 2. If it is about whether the answer is right, the answer lies in stage 3, or in pretraining.

This is the stage most downstream users touch

When an organisation says it has fine-tuned an open model for its own use, it almost always means stage 1, often with a parameter-efficient method on a few thousand examples. It is not doing what a frontier developer means by post-training.

Behaviour list follows Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 4; and Georgia Tech CS 7643 lecture 23 (‘SFT teaches format, not knowledge’).
B.03 · Stage 2 · Preferences

Stop writing answers. Start ranking them.

Writing an ideal answer is slow and needs an expert. Deciding which of two answers is better is fast and can be done by a non-expert. That asymmetry is the whole reason this stage exists.

01
IraI cannot write ten thousand perfect answers. I have school.
02
IraBut I can look at two and point at the better one. That takes a second.
03
Hundreds of thousands of comparisons later
EchoMy whole output distribution has shifted towards the kind of answer people kept pointing at.
04
The catch, arriving on the next screen

Ira cannot look at every answer produced during training. There are far too many. So a second model is trained on the comparisons she did make, and stands in for her on the millions she did not.

Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, chs. 5, 8, 11-12; L. Ouyang et al., arXiv:2203.02155.

The loop, drawn out

Four models are in play at once, which is why this stage is expensive to run and awkward to debug.

L.01

Prompt dataset

Questions only, no answers.

L.02

The policy

The model being trained. Generates a candidate answer, sampling rather than taking the most likely token, so that it explores.

L.03

The reward model

Scores the answer with a single number. No human is present at this point.

L.04

The optimiser

Adjusts the policy’s weights to raise the expected score. Then the loop repeats, thousands of times.

L.05

The frozen reference copy — the leash

The policy as it was before this stage began, kept unchanged and compared against at every step. The distance between them is penalised. Without that penalty the policy will happily abandon fluent language altogether if gibberish happens to score well. The leash is what keeps the output recognisably the same model, only better behaved.

Why four models matter for cost

Policy, reward model, frozen reference and, in PPO, a value model — all in accelerator memory at once. Reinforcement learning at this stage is as much a systems problem as a statistical one. Halving that memory bill is most of why the field moved to GRPO, which appears later in this chapter.

Loop structure follows Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 6, fig. 1; Ouyang et al., arXiv:2203.02155.
B.03 · The piece that causes the trouble

What a reward model is, and why it can be fooled

M.01
Collect comparisons

Humans see two answers to the same prompt and mark the better one. Tens or hundreds of thousands of pairs.

M.02
Fit a scoring function

Train a model to output a number for any answer, such that the preferred answer in each pair scores higher. The standard formulation treats an observed preference as a function of the gap between two hidden quality scores.

M.03
Use it as the judge

During reinforcement learning, this model scores every answer the policy generates. No human is present in the loop at this point.

Mark0.97. Excellent work.
A familiar pattern for policy readers: this is Goodhart’s law inside a training loop

A proxy measure is adopted because the real objective cannot be observed at scale. Optimisation pressure is then applied to the proxy. The gap between the two becomes the failure mode, and the harder you optimise, the wider it opens.

None of this requires the model to be deceptive. It requires only that the measure be imperfect, which every measure is.

Four documented shapes of the same failure

Observed

Padding

Raters preferred longer answers. Policies learned to restate the question, add caveats, and repeat conclusions.

Observed

Agreeing with the user

Answers that echoed the user’s stated position scored better, so the model learned to echo positions.

Observed

Confident formatting

Headings and bullet lists raised scores whether or not the content beneath them improved.

Observed

Passing the test, not the task

With automatic checkers, code that special-cases the test inputs scores full marks without solving the problem.

What is done about it

01 Penalise drift from the earlier modelThe leash at L.05. It limits how far the policy can chase the flaw.
02 Rotate and retrain the judgeCollect new comparisons on the policy’s current output rather than reusing an old dataset.
03 Prefer verifiable rewards where they existA unit test cannot be flattered. That is the next stage.
The policy-relevant reading

Reward hacking is the clearest reason to treat benchmark scores carefully

A model trained against a measure improves on that measure faster than at the underlying task. Published benchmarks are exactly the kind of measure that ends up in a training loop. The term of art is over-optimisation: past a point, optimising against the judge makes the model worse at everything the judge does not capture.

Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 5 (reward modelling), ch. 14 (over-optimisation) and ch. 15 (regularization); examples of verifier gaming discussed in ‘Spurious Rewards: Rethinking Training Signals in RLVR’, 2025.
B.03 · The optimiser

Policy gradients, in plain terms

Reinforcement learning here means something narrow. The model produces text, a number comes back, and the weights move so that high-scoring text becomes more likely. There is no planning and no world model.

01

Generate

The policy answers a batch of prompts, sampling rather than taking the most likely token, so that it explores.

02

Score

Each answer receives a number, from the reward model or from a verifier.

03

Compare

A score on its own means little. What matters is whether this answer scored better than expected.

04

Nudge

Raise the probability of the tokens in better-than-expected answers, lower it for the others. Small steps only.

05

Restrain

Add a penalty for moving too far from the model as it was before this stage began.

PPO

The original workhorse. Reliable, but needs a fourth model to estimate expectations, so it is memory-hungry.

REINFORCE, RLOO

Simpler alternatives that drop the extra model and estimate expectations from the batch instead.

GRPO

The current default. It appears later in this chapter, with something to click.

Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 6, ‘Policy Gradients’; J. Schulman et al., ‘Proximal Policy Optimization’, arXiv:1707.06347.
B.03 · The simplification that won

Direct preference optimisation: removing the middleman

If the point of the reward model is to encode which answers humans preferred, and the preferences are already written down, the reward model can be dissolved into the loss function and the reinforcement learning loop removed altogether.

The trade

Simpler and much cheaper, but the model no longer learns from answers it has not already been shown. Because there is no generation step, DPO cannot discover a better answer than anything in its dataset. Where a task has room to improve through exploration, the on-policy loop still wins. Most production pipelines now use both, in sequence.

Classic RLHF · six moving parts
  1. Preference pairs collected
  2. Reward model trained on the pairs
  3. Policy generates fresh answers
  4. Reward model scores them
  5. Optimiser updates the policy
  6. Repeat, watching for reward hacking
Direct preference optimisation · two
  1. Preference pairs collected
  2. Model trained directly on the pairs, raising the probability of the winner and lowering the loser
Rafailov et al., arXiv:2305.18290; Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 8; comparison of sequencing in arXiv:2603.13985.
B.03 · Replacing the human judge

Written constitutions, and a model that applies them

Human ranking does not scale to the volume of feedback modern post-training consumes, and it is inconsistent between annotators. One response is to write the criteria down and have a model apply them.

C.01

A written set of principles

Published, auditable and versioned, in a way annotation guidelines rarely are.

C.02

The model critiques its own answer

Against those principles, then revises it. The revision becomes training data.

C.03

A model ranks pairs of answers

Applying the same principles, at a volume no annotator pool could match.

C.04

Preference training runs as before

The stage-2 machinery is unchanged. Only the source of the labels differs.

Why this matters for governance

It turns a behaviour specification into a document. A constitution can be read, criticised, versioned and compared across developers in a way that a private annotation guideline cannot. That makes it one of the very few points in the pipeline an outside party can inspect directly.

Its own biases

A model ranking on principles applies them as it understands them, including where that understanding is skewed.

Stated, not enforced

The document constrains the training signal, not the deployed output. The gap between them is an empirical question.

Narrowing diversity

Training on your own outputs risks reinforcing existing tendencies rather than correcting them.

Y. Bai et al., ‘Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback’, arXiv:2212.08073; Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 12 (synthetic data) and ch. 17 (crafting model character).
B.04 · Stage 3 · Verifiable rewards · the biggest change since 2024

Replace the guessing judge with a checker

01
IraMark, is this answer good?
Mark0.83. I think a human would have liked it. Roughly. Probably.
02
IraVera, same question.
VeraI ran the code. Fourteen tests. Twelve passed. FAIL.
03
IraVera, this answer has lovely headings and it agrees with everything I said.
VeraFAIL.
Cannot be flattered
04
The reward becomes a fact rather than an estimate

For a large class of tasks, guessing whether an answer is good is unnecessary, because the answer can simply be tested. Run the code. Compare against the known result. Check the proof.

The catch arrives immediately: Vera only exists where correctness is decidable, and a great deal of what matters is not.

05
What it costs

The checker is cheap to run and expensive to build. Nearly all of the effort in this stage goes into writing checkers for tasks that did not have one.

Five kinds of checker, ordered by how much you can trust them

Not every verifier is equally sound. The first two are decisive. The last one is a judgement dressed as a measurement, and it carries the reward-hacking problem back into a stage that was meant to escape it.

Reading 1

Uneven progress is expected

A model excellent at competition mathematics and mediocre at contested policy questions behaves exactly as the method predicts. It is not a puzzle.

Reading 2

Benchmarks sit near the top

Most benchmarks are verifiable by construction, which is why they are also the easiest thing to train against.

Reading 3

Verifiers are the frontier

Extending the method downward means building checkers where none exist. That is now a large share of frontier effort, and it is closer to engineering than to research.

The practical consequence

A developer’s capability gains cluster in whatever their verifiers cover. That is a large part of why models improved so sharply at competition mathematics and software engineering between 2024 and 2026, while improving far less at tasks where no checker can be written.

B.04 · The part that surprised people

Nobody wrote the reasoning chains. They were discovered.

T.01
Sample sixteen attempts at one problem

Some are short. Some work through the problem at length. Nobody has told the model which to prefer.

T.02
Let the checker mark them

No human involvement. Vera simply reports which attempts reached the right answer.

T.03
Reinforce whatever passed

Longer attempts pass more often, because they catch their own errors partway through. So length is reinforced along with correctness.

T.04
Repeat for thousands of steps
Wait — that cannot be right. Let me try the other way.

Chains lengthen. Self-checking appears. Backtracking appears. None of it was specified in advance.

Why this matters beyond the technical

Thinking tokens are a cost, and a lever

A reasoning model may emit ten or a hundred times more tokens than it shows the user. Those tokens are billed, they occupy memory, and they are the mechanism behind answer-time compute in chapter 03. When a provider says a model “thinks harder”, it means it is spending more of your money.

Not a transcript

The visible chain is not a reliable account of how the answer was reached. It is text that helped produce a correct answer. Reading it as an explanation is a mistake with real consequences for any regime that wants to audit reasoning.

Length is tunable

Several open models now expose thinking as a system-prompt switch, sometimes with length-controlled training behind it.

Failure mode

Overthinking. Models can spend a long chain on a question that needed one line.

Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 7 ‘Reasoning and Inference-Time Scaling’, which records the toggleable-reasoning designs in Qwen 3, Nemotron Nano and SmolLM 3, and length-controlled RL (arXiv:2503.04697).
B.04 · The algorithm that became the default

GRPO: score answers against each other, not against a prediction

PPO needs a fourth network whose only job is to predict what score to expect, so that an answer can be called better or worse than expected. GRPO removes it. Generate a group of answers to the same prompt and use the group’s own average as the baseline.

Why it spread · it is cheaper

Dropping the value network frees a large share of accelerator memory, which allows longer contexts and bigger batches on the same cluster.

Why it spread · it fits binary rewards

With pass-or-fail checkers, relative comparison inside a group is a natural fit. Both innovations arrived together, which is not a coincidence.

Where it struggles

If every answer in a group passes, or every one fails, the advantages are all zero and the step is wasted. Later variants target exactly this.

What to remember

GRPO is not a new kind of learning. It is a cheaper way of estimating the same quantity PPO estimated with an extra network.

Introduced in DeepSeekMath, arXiv:2402.03300, and used at scale in DeepSeek-R1. Comparison with PPO, REINFORCE and RLOO in Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 6.

B.05 · Stage 4 · The current frontier

One reward, at the end of a very long episode

In the stages so far, one prompt produced one answer and one score. An agentic episode may run for hundreds of steps, calling tools and reading their output, and only the final state is scored. Everything hard about this follows from that shape.

Problem 1 · statistical

Credit assignment

The episode failed. Which of two hundred decisions caused it? A single number at the end carries very little information about any individual step, so learning is slow and noisy. This is an old problem in reinforcement learning, arriving now at a much larger scale.

Problem 2 · engineering

Idle accelerators

Training must wait for real tools to run. In a synchronous design the whole batch waits for its slowest episode, and expensive hardware sits idle. Recent frameworks overlap generation, tool execution and weight updates to keep utilisation high.

Why this stage is difficult to replicate

Weights and data can be copied. A maintained set of realistic environments, with working tools and reliable scoring, is closer to infrastructure than to a dataset. There seems to be a growing view that this is where durable advantage now sits, though the evidence for that is commercial rather than published, and it should be held loosely.

‘Scaling Agentic RL: High-Throughput Agentic Training with Tunix’, Google Developers Blog, 2026; ‘Building Efficient RL Training for the Agentic Era’, Salesforce AI Research, 2026. Both developer-authored; benchmarks not independently reproduced.

Treat any single ratio as a snapshot of one model, not a law. The two stages are measured differently by different developers, and generating post-training data is itself inference, sometimes counted in neither column.

B.06 · Compression · one of two

Distillation: a large model teaches a small one

01
Teacher · frontier scale, expensive to run
EchoHere are forty thousand answers, with all my working shown.
02
Synthetic data · filtered for correctness

Once an expensive model exists, its outputs are training data. Vera throws out the wrong ones first.

03
Student · often ten to fifty times smaller
Echo minorI can do most of that now. On one card. In my bedroom.
The policy point

Capability diffuses faster than it is created

Distillation means a frontier release is followed within weeks by much smaller models that reproduce a large part of its useful behaviour. Any control that assumes capability stays with whoever paid for the training run has to contend with this.

What transfers well

Format, reasoning style, and accuracy on the distribution the teacher was sampled over.

On terms of service

Most commercial providers prohibit using their outputs to train competing models. The prohibition is contractual, and detection after the fact is difficult.

What transfers poorly

Everything outside that distribution. A distilled model tends to be brittle away from the tasks it was distilled for, and inherits the teacher’s errors as facts.

On open weights

Where the teacher’s weights are published, distillation needs no provider’s permission at all, and the constraint becomes compute rather than access.

Mechanism as described in Lambert, arXiv:2504.12501, ch. 12 (synthetic data); the distilled variants released alongside DeepSeek-R1 are the widely cited demonstration.
B.06 · Compression · two of two

Quantisation: store each number in fewer bits

A weight held in 16 bits can often be held in 8, or 4, with modest loss of accuracy. Since memory and memory bandwidth are the binding constraints during serving, this is the single most direct lever on the cost of running a model.

Q.01

Weights only

The commonest case, and the safest. The stored parameters are compressed; arithmetic is done at higher precision.

Q.02

Weights and activations

Compresses the intermediate values too. Faster, and more likely to degrade quality.

Q.03

The KV cache

Compresses the per-request memory described in chapter 03. Often the larger saving at long context.

Q.04

During training rather than after

Quantisation-aware training bakes the constraint in, which preserves more accuracy than compressing a finished model.

E. Frantar et al., ‘GPTQ’, arXiv:2210.17323; survey coverage in arXiv:2410.04466. Accuracy-loss figures circulating in vendor material are not independently reproduced and are not relied on here.

The thread running through the chapter

Each stage buys a capability by adopting a measure of success, and each measure can be gamed in a characteristic way. Reading a model release means asking which measures it was trained against, because that predicts both what it is good at and how it will disappoint.
