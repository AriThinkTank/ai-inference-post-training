03

The Bill Arrives

Nothing in this chapter changes the model. It's finished. The only question left is what it costs to run, and the answer explains most of an API price list.

One sentence does most of the work here: generating a word is limited by how fast memory moves, not by how fast the chip calculates. That sounds like an engineering detail. It's the reason output tokens cost more than input tokens, the reason long context is expensive, and the reason half the tricks below exist.

C.02

Five numbers, and two of them fight each other

These turn up constantly in vendor material and are almost never defined. Learn them and most performance claims become readable.

Time to first token

How long you stare at nothing. Set by prefill, so it grows with your prompt.

Time per output token

The gap between one word appearing and the next. This is what makes a reply feel quick or laboured.

Throughput

Tokens per second across everybody on the machine. The operator's number, not yours.

Batch size

How many people share the accelerator. Raise it and cost falls while everyone waits longer.

Goodput

Throughput counting only the requests that actually hit their latency target. The honest one.

Speed and cost are bought from each other

Pack more requests onto a chip and the cost per token drops while every individual user waits longer. Where a provider sets that dial is a commercial decision, and they never tell you where.

The question to askAt what batch size, and at what context length? A throughput figure without both is not comparable to anything. A surprising number of published figures come without both.
Definitions follow Austin et al., ch. 7, and arXiv:2410.04466.

C.04

Four ways to build attention, chosen once and locked in forever

Attention needs a lot of different questions asked of the text, but far fewer copies of the text itself. Sharing keys and values exploits that gap. It's decided before training and fixes the serving cost for the model's entire life.

C.05

Three tricks, two of them stolen from operating systems

01
IraJust give each conversation the memory it needs.
02
EchoNobody knows how long a reply will be until it's over. Early systems booked room for the longest reply that might happen. Most replies are short, so most of that very expensive memory sat empty.
03 Solved in 1962

Operating systems hit this decades ago. Stop demanding memory in one lump. Cut it into fixed pages, scatter them anywhere, keep a lookup table. Somebody finally applied it to attention in 2023 and got two to four times the throughput.

Kwon et al., SOSP 2023, arXiv:2309.06180. The two-to-four-fold figure is the authors' own measurement. It is now standard in every serious engine, so it's a floor rather than an advantage.
Timelines are schematic. Mechanism: Yu et al., Orca, OSDI 2022; Agrawal et al., Sarathi-Serve, OSDI 2024.

The one that shows up on your invoice

Loads of requests start the same way. A system prompt, a policy document, a codebase, the earlier turns of a chat. Reading that shared opening produces identical results every time, so it gets read once and reused. That's prefix caching, and it's why the cached portion is billed at a fraction of the rate.

Agents would be impossible without this

An agent replays a growing transcript on every single step. Without prefix caching the same history gets re-read dozens of times in one task, and a long agent run costs a fortune. This single optimisation is what makes the entire product category commercially viable.

One thing to be careful about

Shared caches mean two requests can point at the same blocks. That's fine when they're yours. It raises real isolation questions when they belong to different customers, and providers vary in how they handle it.

Built on the block sharing in arXiv:2309.06180. Pricing structures vary by provider. Check current documentation rather than assuming.

01

02
IraWhat if something small and quick guessed the next few words and you just checked them?
03
EchoI can check four guesses inside a sweep I was making anyway. Three were right. I'll redo the fourth.
04 Free speed

Speculative decoding provably gives you the exact answer the big model would have written on its own, just sooner. No quality is traded away. There's a proof.

Helps when

The chip is underused. Small batches, one person at a time, interactive use.

Hurts when

The chip is already flat out. At large batch sizes it adds work without adding speed, so it usually gets switched off above a batch size in the low tens.

Changes nothing else

A rejected guess is simply redone. Nobody, including the model, could tell from the output that it happened.

Leviathan et al., arXiv:2211.17192. The batch-size guidance comes from vendor engineering guides and has not been independently reproduced.

How to read a parameter count

In a mixture-of-experts model the bulk of the network is split into many blocks, and a small router sends each token to just a couple of them. So total parameters and active parameters come apart, and every headline quotes the first.

Why this matters for compute assessmentsCompare a mixture-of-experts model to a dense one on total parameters and you'll overstate its compute per token and understate the machine it needs. A model can be cheap to run and still require a very large box to hold it. Any assessment should say which number it's using.
Austin et al., ch. 4, question 8, and ch. 7.
C.07

Or you can pay more on purpose

Mode 1
Think for longer
Let me check that. No, wrong. Back to line three.

The model writes out its working before answering, correcting itself as it goes. This is the behaviour that appeared on its own in chapter 02.

You pay for the working, even when it's hidden from you
Mode 2
Answer several times
try 1try 2try 3try 4

Take several independent shots and pick between them, by vote, by a scoring model, or by running a checker over each.

Costs multiply. Waiting time doesn't, because they run at once
Mode 3
Use tools and try again

Search, run some code, read a file, revise. The loop can go round many times.

Cost is unknowable in advance, which is exactly why agent products are hard to price

Capability isn't a property of the weights any more

It's a property of the weights and the budget you allow at the moment of asking. Same file, very different behaviour. And the budget is set by whoever deploys the model, not whoever trained it.

Answer-time compute does buy real accuracy, with diminishing returns, and unevenly across kinds of task.

So what does a benchmark score mean?Less than you'd hope. It's a score for a model at one budget setting, and the setting is almost never published. Chapter 04 takes this apart.
Lambert, rlhfbook.com, ch. 7; scaling curves as published with OpenAI's o1 and discussed in arXiv:2410.04466, sec. 5.2.
