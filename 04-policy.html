The Instruments Were Built for a Different Picture

Compute controls were designed around a world where capability got made once, in one enormous run, on hardware you could see. Every part of that sentence is now weaker than it was.

Four consequences follow, and one thing that doesn't follow. None of this is a recommendation, and the last section lists what we're least sure about.

D.01

Three estimates that disagree, and why

Everyone agrees inference is now a big share of AI compute. Nobody agrees on how big, mostly because they aren't counting the same things.

One firm, 2024
~30%

Epoch AI's estimate for one developer's spending. One company, and a year before reasoning models were widely deployed.

The whole industry, 2026
~2/3

A consultancy forecast, reported second-hand, tracing a path from a third in 2023 to a half in 2025. Treat with care.

The sturdy one
54–62%

Share of operating costs going to compute at the firms Epoch can model. Research and inference together, and the figure least damaged by the counting problem.

Why 1
Different denominators

One company's spending against the world's installed capacity. Frontier labs are thought to use under half of global AI compute.

Why 2
A boundary problem

The rollouts that reinforcement learning burns through are inference runs. Sometimes they get counted in neither column.

this is the big one
Why 3
Different source quality

Epoch publishes its method and its data. The industry-wide number is a forecast reported second-hand.

So Ask for the denominator

Inference is a large and rising share. Any percentage needs its source and its denominator attached. A figure quoted without both shouldn't be carrying weight in an argument.

Epoch AI, Trends dashboard and Data Insights, February 2026; Epoch AI, "Frontier labs don't use most AI compute (yet)", May 2026; Deloitte 2026 TMT Predictions as reported in trade coverage, not independently verified.

Purvapaksha

The strongest case against everything above

Very large pretraining runs are still hard to hide and impossible without advanced accelerators. Hardware remains the most observable input in the entire system. Nothing in the first three chapters changes either of those facts, and both are load-bearing for the case that controls work.

The narrower claim we'd defend: the share of capability those controls reach appears to be falling, and anything measured only at training time now misses more than it did. That's a different argument, and it deserves to be argued separately.

A test for your own writingIf a sentence would still be true had post-training never been invented, it's probably safe. If it depends on capability arriving in one measurable lump, check it against this table first.

D.03

Capability travels much faster than it gets made

Three mechanisms from chapter 02, working together, squeeze the gap between a frontier release and something roughly similar being widely available.

One

Distillation

A frontier model's answers, reasoning traces included, train much smaller models to do a good deal of the same work.

Two

Published methods

Everything in chapter 02 is in the open literature within months. The algorithms were never the hard part. Sequence and data quality are.

Three

Quantisation

Once a capable open model exists, compression walks it down onto cheaper and cheaper hardware, and eventually onto machines nobody is watching.

What this suggests for a country in India's position

The binding constraint may not be access to weights at all. The harder questions are whether the complements exist: accelerators to serve inference at national scale, engineering depth at layer 4 of the serving stack, and verifiable-reward environments for the domains that matter here.

That's our read of the technical picture, not a measured finding, and it's offered as such.

The honest qualificationDiffusion is shallower than the headlines suggest. A distilled model matches its teacher on what it was distilled for and falls away sharply outside that. Agentic ability, which needs maintained environments rather than weights, has spread far more slowly. Ask anyone claiming everything diffuses in weeks about environments.
D.04

There isn't a fixed thing to evaluate any more

The same weights, given different answer-time budgets, behave like different systems. That's awkward for any rule attached to a model rather than to a deployment.

Setting A

Bare minimum

Capability 

No extended thinking, one attempt, no tools.

Setting B

Let it think

Capability 

A long chain of working before it answers.

Setting C

Everything on

Capability 

Many attempts, a checker, a search loop, retries.

Same weights throughout. Bar lengths are illustrative and are not measurements. The dial itself is on chapter 03.
For evaluation

A benchmark result belongs to a model and a budget. Results published without the budget aren't comparable to each other, and the budget is almost never published.

For disclosure

A safety evaluation run at one budget says little about behaviour at ten times that budget, which a deployer can simply switch on.

For thresholds

A rule written over training compute leaves answer-time compute unbounded. How far one substitutes for the other is not well understood.

A constructive reading

This also points at something cheap and useful

Put the disclosure on the deployment configuration rather than on the weights. Publishing the answer-time budget alongside a benchmark result would cost a developer almost nothing and would make published figures comparable for the first time. An option, not a recommendation.

D.05

Where this site is on thinnest ice

Chapters 02 and 03 mix published research, developer disclosure and vendor claims of wildly varying quality. These are the four places we'd expect to be wrong.

U.01 Whether reinforcement learning creates ability or just uncovers it Actively contested in 2026. If it mostly elicits what's already there, the case for controlling pretraining compute is stronger than this site implies.
U.02 How far answer-time compute substitutes for training compute Both help. The exchange rate between them isn't characterised, and it almost certainly differs by task.
U.03 The real training and inference split No reliable public series exists. The estimates on this page differ by a factor of two and aren't measuring the same quantity.
U.04 Serving performance figures Most 2026 numbers on batching, caching and speculation come from the vendors whose systems they describe. Nobody has reproduced them.
How sources were graded

Three tiers

Peer-reviewed papers and primary technical reports: trusted for mechanism. Developer blogs: trusted for what that developer did, not for comparison. Consultancy forecasts and vendor benchmarks: named, and never load-bearing.

On the frameworks

Organising devices, not findings

The four teachers, the verifiability ladder and the six-layer stack were assembled for the underlying deck. They're ways of arranging what's known. No claim to novelty.

On U.01: contested in the 2026 literature. On U.03: Epoch AI, February 2026, against Deloitte 2026 TMT Predictions.
If nothing else

Three questions worth asking out loud

01

A model gets released

What was it trained against? That predicts its strengths and its disappointments, and you can usually find it in the release notes.

02

Someone quotes a benchmark

At what answer-time budget? A number without a budget can't be compared to another number without a budget.

03

Someone quotes a compute figure

What got counted, and what got left out? The reinforcement learning rollouts are the usual missing column.

Corrections welcome, and most welcome on the four points above.
