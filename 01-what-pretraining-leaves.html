Chapter 01 · What pretraining leaves behind

The Next-Token Machine

A base model is a very good text-continuation engine and a very poor assistant. Knowing exactly which capabilities are present at the end of pretraining, and which are absent, is what makes everything after it legible.

A.01 · The base model

It scores every possible next word, and then somebody picks one

01
IraWhat are you thinking about right now?
02
EchoNothing. I have five words. I am producing a number for each of the 128,000 words that could come sixth.
03
IraAnd then?
EchoSomebody picks one. It gets added to the end. Then I do it again.
04
Nothing inside a base model knows about questions, answers, users or helpfulness.

There is no plan, no draft, no intention to be useful. There is a sequence of tokens in, and a probability for every token that could come next. Text appears by picking one, appending it, and repeating. That is the whole mechanism.

Again. And again.
05
Why this matters for everything that follows

Every technique in the next two chapters either reshapes that list of numbers, computes it more cheaply, or draws from it more times before settling on a reply. There is nothing else to do.

Mechanism: A. Vaswani et al., ‘Attention Is All You Need’, arXiv:1706.03762; J. Austin et al., How To Scale Your Model, ch. 4 (jax-ml.github.io/scaling-book). Dialogue is invented.
The grown-up version

One turn of the machine, in three steps

Step 1

The prompt, broken into tokens

India·has·a·large·ethanol

A token is a fragment of text, roughly three-quarters of an English word. Not a character and not a word. Everything the model reads, writes and is billed for is counted in these.

Step 2

The forward pass

Hundreds of billions of arithmetic operations, arranged as layers of attention and feed-forward blocks. This is the expensive part, and chapter 03 is entirely about making it cheaper.

Nothing in this step is aware of what it is producing. It is matrix multiplication all the way down.

Step 3

A probability for every token

programme31%
blending24%
industry17%
sector11%
policy6%
128,000 others11%
Probabilities are illustrative, not measured. They are the figures used in the underlying briefing deck, chosen to show the shape of a typical distribution.
Try it · A.01a

Cut a sentence up the way a model sees it

Long words break into pieces. Numbers break into digits. A space belongs to the word after it. This is why a model can spell badly, why some languages cost more per sentence than others, and why your bill is measured in a unit you never chose.

Try it · A.01b

Turn the dials on the probability list

Temperature flattens or sharpens the list before a token is drawn. Top-k keeps the best few. Top-p keeps as many as it takes to cover a share of the probability. Push temperature past 1.5 and watch the 128,000 unnamed tokens swallow the list, which is exactly what happens in a real model and exactly why nobody serves at that setting.

A.02 · Vocabulary

Four words that describe the machine, not the model

Token

A fragment of text, roughly three-quarters of an English word. The unit of everything: context, billing, memory.

Temperature

How flat the list is made before a token is drawn. Low values almost always pick the top entry. It is a knob on the sampling, not a change to the model.

Sampling

The rule for drawing from the list: greedy, top-k, top-p, or beam search. Two people asking the same model the same question can get different answers because of this alone.

Context

How many earlier tokens may be attended to. Every one of them costs memory during serving, which chapter 03 works out in gigabytes.

A.03 · The handover

What is already in the box, and what has to be added

This is the most useful list on the site. The five on the left arrive free with pretraining and cannot easily be added later. The five on the right are absent at the handover, and every one of them is the work of chapter 02.

Present · acquired from the corpus

01 Broad factual and world knowledgeAlmost everything the model will ever know about the world arrives at this stage.
02 Fluent language, in many languagesGrammar, register, idiom, and the ability to move between them.
03 In-context learningPicking up a pattern from examples inside the prompt, with no weight change at all.
04 Latent reasoning abilityThe capacity is there but unreliable and hard to elicit on demand.
05 Some code and mathematicsLearned incidentally from the corpus rather than taught deliberately.

Absent · must be added afterwards

06 Answering rather than continuingGiven a question, a base model may produce five more questions. That is a faithful continuation of its corpus.
07 Knowing when to stopNothing in pretraining teaches a natural end to a reply.
08 Refusal and safety behaviourThe corpus contains harmful text, and a base model reproduces it on request.
09 Tool and function-call syntaxEmitting a structured call that an external system can execute.
10 Reliable long-form reasoningWorking through a problem in steps, on demand, and checking the result.

A.04 · A claim worth tracking

The claim that post-training only teaches manners

01
The superficial alignment hypothesis, as originally stated
Models acquire nearly all their knowledge during pretraining. Post-training does not teach new knowledge. It teaches the model which format to use when expressing knowledge it already holds.
02
IraSo it is just table manners? It already knew everything, it just did not know how to say it?
03
EchoThat was a fair reading in 2023. A few thousand good examples were enough to turn me into something usable.
04
EchoIt is doing less work than it used to. Nobody wrote down the long chains of self-correcting working I now produce. Those came out of being marked by a program, thousands of times.

How to grade the claim

The hypothesis has not been refuted. It has been narrowed.

Knowledge does still seem to arrive almost entirely during pretraining. What has changed is the claim about capability. Eliciting a latent ability reliably, at length, and under verification now looks less like formatting and more like teaching a skill.

The distinction is not academic. It decides whether pretraining compute is the right quantity to measure or control — which is the argument of chapter 04.

R.01

Behaviour that was not in the data

Long chains of self-correcting working emerged from reinforcement learning against automatic checkers. No human wrote those chains as demonstrations.

R.02

The compute share has moved

Post-training has gone from a rounding error to a large and rising fraction of the compute used to build a frontier model.

R.03

Gains now attributed to it

Developers increasingly attribute benchmark movement between releases to post-training rather than to a larger base model.

Open question · actively contested

Does reinforcement learning add capability, or only sharpen access to capability already present?

Several 2026 papers argue the effect is largely elicitation from a sparse existing subnetwork. The answer bears directly on how much a small actor can achieve with a strong open base model, and on whether a control aimed at pretraining compute reaches the thing it is aimed at. This site does not settle it, and neither does anyone else yet.

Hypothesis as summarised in Georgia Tech CS 7643 lecture 23. Contested in, among others, ‘The Multiple Ticket Hypothesis: Random Sparse Subnetworks Suffice for RLVR’, arXiv:2602.01599.
End of chapter 01

A fluent machine with no idea what it is for

Everything the model knows, it now knows. What it lacks is the habit of answering, the sense to stop, the judgement to refuse, and the discipline to work through a problem. Four teachers are about to supply those, one at a time, and each of them can be fooled in its own characteristic way.
