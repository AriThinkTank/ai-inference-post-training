The Next-Token Machine

A hundred million dollars buys you a machine that knows a staggering amount and won't answer a single question.

This chapter is about what that machine actually does, and about the gap between knowing something and being willing to say it. Two things to play with: a tokeniser, and the sampler that decides which word comes out.

Everything a language model appears to do sits on top of this loop. Reasoning, translating, writing code, refusing a request. All of it is scoring the next token and picking one, a few hundred times a second.

the model never chooses. it only scores.
05
Worth holding on to

The picking happens outside the model, in a few lines of code you can change without retraining anything. That code is the next section.

The loop, in three steps

Step 1

Chop it up

Your sentence becomes a list of numbers. Not words, not letters. Something in between, called tokens.

Step 2

One sweep

The whole list passes through every layer of the model once. This is the expensive part.

Step 3

A score for everything

Out comes one number for every token in the vocabulary. These are the logits. Not an answer, and not yet probabilities.

Why tokens and not words?The model needs a fixed vocabulary, and English alone has millions of words. Tokens are the compromise. Type into the box below and you'll see the seams.

A.02

Four dials that sit outside the model

None of these change a single weight. All four change what you get back, which is why the same model can feel sharp on Monday and vague on Tuesday.

Dial 1

Temperature

Every score gets divided by it. Below 1 stretches the gaps apart. Above 1 squashes them together. Watch it happen.

Dial 2

The sampling rule

Top-k keeps a fixed headcount. Top-p keeps as many as it takes to cover a share of the probability, so the count changes with the model’s confidence.

Dial 3

The system prompt

Text you never see, pasted in front of yours. Cheapest way to change behaviour, and the easiest to talk a model out of.

Dial 4

Context length

How much it holds at once. Sold as a feature, priced like one. Chapter 03 explains why it costs what it does.

Why this matters when you read a benchmark claim

Two labs can publish different numbers for the same weights simply because they set these dials differently. Almost nobody reports the settings.

A.03

The handover: what pretraining leaves behind

An enormous amount of knowledge, and almost no manners. Have a guess at the ten items below before you check.

The pattern, once you've done all ten

Facts, grammar, code syntax, the shape of an argument, a rough sense of how the world works: all there. Answering when asked, stopping when finished, refusing when it should, admitting it doesn't know: all missing.

Knowledge comes from reading. Behaviour has to be taught, and that teaching is chapter 02.

Something odd about base modelsAsk one the same question three times and you may get three different answers, all confident, all in different formats. Consistency is a taught behaviour too.

A.04

One argument nobody has settled

01
The claim, made in 2023

Post-training teaches a model nothing new. It only teaches it which of the things it already knows to say out loud. Call it the surface hypothesis.

02
IraSo all that expensive teaching is just… manners?
03
Evidence for

A thousand carefully chosen examples get you most of the way. The knowledge clearly isn't arriving at that point. And the same base model can be aligned in wildly different directions.

04
Evidence against

Reasoning models trained with verifiable rewards solve problems their base models couldn't solve at any temperature, with any number of tries. That looks like new ability, not better manners.

still being argued about in 2026
Why you should care

The compute-control argument rests on this

If post-training only reveals what pretraining already built, then watching the big pretraining runs catches most of what matters. If it genuinely creates ability, a lot of capability is now being made in runs too small and too numerous to watch.

This site leans towards the second reading, and flags where that lean is doing work. Mostly in chapter 04.

The original claim

LIMA and the superficial alignment hypothesis, arXiv:2305.11206.

The counter-evidence

Reasoning gains under RLVR, in DeepSeek-R1.

Where it stands

Contested. Treat anyone who says it's obvious with suspicion, in either direction.
