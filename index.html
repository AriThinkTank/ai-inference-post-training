Three Stages, Three Problems

Follow the money

Building an AI model, teaching it to behave, and running it for users are three different businesses. They buy different things, from different people, and only one of them is genuinely vulnerable to export controls.

This dashboard works out where the money goes at each stage, which suppliers hold each line of the bill, and what that means for a country in India's position. No algorithms are explained. The technical detail sits underneath the numbers rather than on the page.

The one-line versionPretraining buys hardware. Post-training buys people, up to a point we can calculate. Inference buys chips, buildings and power in roughly equal measure. Only some of that can be restricted, and we have tested each claim against independent sources.

The finding

Same technology, completely different bills

Stage 1
Pretraining · about $350m
47–65% hardware

Most of the bill buys chips, servers and the fabric that wires them together, all of which need somebody else's permission. The range is wide because the published cost model puts research staff anywhere between 29 and 49 per cent of the total, and hardware takes whatever is left. Counting the data line too, which is partly restricted, 66 per cent of the bill is reached by some control instrument.

the stage everyone regulates
Stage 2
Post-training · about $8m
80%, then it flips

At the scale India would realistically work at, four-fifths of the bill buys human judgement and nobody licenses people. But push reinforcement learning hard enough and compute takes over. The crossing point is at roughly half of pretraining compute, and the frontier labs have passed it.

the stage nobody regulates
Stage 3
Inference · the recurring bill
39–47% domestic

Chips still lead, but power, buildings and staff are well over a third of the cost, and the chips here can be older ones. Four independent estimates agree within eight points, which makes this the best-supported of the three claims.

A note on the two numbersPost-training appears here as 80 per cent untouchable and elsewhere as 70 per cent people and environments. Those are the same cost stack counted to different edges: 45 human data plus 25 environments is the 70, and adding the 10 for a small research team gives the 80. Neither figure holds at frontier reinforcement learning intensity, which is the correction on the workings page.
Why this matters more than it soundsExport controls were written for stage one, where the money is in hardware. The stage that decides who can afford to use AI spends nearly half its budget on power and buildings that no instrument reaches. And the stage that decides what a model can actually do spends most of its budget on people, at least until somebody starts running reinforcement learning at frontier scale. The policy conversation assumes one cost structure. There are three, and one of them moves.

Sources as listed in stage-costs.csv. The V3 and R1 figures are compute only and exclude staff and data, which is exactly why the next page matters.

Hold those last two numbers together

The compute for R1's reinforcement learning cost less than $300,000. A serious set of expert preference comparisons for the same model would cost several million. At this stage the humans cost more than the machines, by a wide margin, and that inverts everything people assume about AI being a capital-intensive business.

Which is the interesting bit for India

India cannot buy its way to the top of stage one. It has more of what stage two actually needs than almost anyone, and it is not currently spending on it.
