Three Stages, Three Problems

Follow the money

Building an AI model, teaching it to behave, and running it for users are three different businesses. They buy different things, from different people, and only one of them is genuinely vulnerable to export controls.

This dashboard works out where the money goes at each stage, which suppliers hold each line of the bill, and what that means for a country in India's position. No algorithms are explained. The technical detail sits underneath the numbers rather than on the page.

The one-line versionPretraining buys hardware. Post-training buys people. Inference buys buildings and power. Only the first is easy to restrict.

The finding

Same technology, completely different bills

Stage 1
Pretraining · about $350m
62% restricted

Two-thirds of the bill buys chips, servers and the fabric that wires them together. All of it needs somebody else's permission, and there is no second source for the part that matters.

the stage everyone regulates
Stage 2
Post-training · about $8m
80% untouchable

Four-fifths of the bill buys human judgement: expert comparisons, graded test environments, small research teams. Nobody licenses people. No export control reaches any of it.

the stage nobody regulates
Stage 3
Inference · the recurring bill
43% domestic

Chips still lead, but power, buildings and staff are more than two-fifths of the cost, and the chips can be older ones. This is where most of the industry's money now goes.

Why this matters more than it soundsExport controls were written for stage one, where the money is in hardware. But the stage that decides what a model can actually do, and the stage that decides who can afford to use it, both spend most of their budget on things no control instrument can touch. The policy conversation and the cost structure have come apart.

$294k

The entire reinforcement learning stage. 147,000 GPU-hours.

Sources as listed in stage-costs.csv. The V3 and R1 figures are compute only and exclude staff and data, which is exactly why the next page matters.

Hold those last two numbers together

The compute for R1's reinforcement learning cost less than $300,000. A serious set of expert preference comparisons for the same model would cost several million. At this stage the humans cost more than the machines, by a wide margin, and that inverts everything people assume about AI being a capital-intensive business.

Which is the interesting bit for India

India cannot buy its way to the top of stage one. It has more of what stage two actually needs than almost anyone, and it is not currently spending on it.
